Let me break down a battle plan for making the transition between your horizontal pano and the top/bottom views look seamless, fluid, and never noticeable:

🎯 Step 1. Smart Scene Structure

Wrap your horizontal pano on the inside of a large sphere (THREE.SphereGeometry).

The top and bottom aren’t really part of that pano — instead, you’ll use separate textures for them:

A hemisphere/dome cap at the top for sky/ceiling.

A hemisphere cap at the bottom for ground/floor.

This way, horizontal and vertical aren’t “stitched” crudely — they’re layered geometry that blend.

🎯 Step 2. Gradient Fade Zones

The key trick: don’t let the pano just “end” at the top/bottom.

Apply a shader or gradient mask on the pano texture’s poles:

Top fades from pano → transparent.

Bottom fades from pano → transparent.

Then behind it, your sky/floor textures show through.
👉 This creates the illusion that the pano is connected to the caps.

🎯 Step 3. Color Matching & Blending

Sample the average color of the top/bottom edges of your pano.

Tint the sky/floor caps with that color (like Google Street View does).

Optionally blur the edge textures so they don’t clash with sharp pano edges.

🎯 Step 4. Smooth Drag Logic

Right now, when you drag upward, the transition from horizontal → top feels abrupt.

Fix this by easing camera rotation speed as you approach the pano’s vertical limit:

controls.minPolarAngle = Math.PI/3;  
controls.maxPolarAngle = 2*Math.PI/3;  


Add a fade mix:

As the user drags up, increase opacity of the sky cap while decreasing opacity of the pano top edge.

Vice versa for dragging down to the floor.

This way, the transition is cross-faded, not cut.

🎯 Step 5. Limit Exposure to Bad Data

Panorama edges (especially top/bottom) always look “clustered” because there’s no real data there.

The solution:

Clamp the vertical view at ~85° instead of letting users go full 90°.

This way, they almost see the ceiling/floor but never the ugly distortion.

The caps fill in the missing realism.

🎯 Step 6. Motion Illusion (Google Trick)

Google Maps never lets you see “raw texture limits.” Instead, it:

Slightly distorts/morphs the pano at the poles.

Adds a soft black vignette fade near extremes.

Moves the exposure/brightness dynamically so the transition feels natural.

You can replicate this by:

Adding a radial vignette shader at the screen edges.

Slightly scaling/warping the pano mesh at the poles.

🎯 Step 7. Mobile/Tactile Improvements

Phones should have:

Touch drag rotation (with inertia, so motion continues briefly after release).

On-screen joystick for users who prefer controls.

Both should obey the same fade logic so the transition is identical across devices.

✅ Result if you do all these:
When dragging up or down, the pano gracefully fades into a top/bottom “cap” that is color-matched, blurred, and blended.
The user never sees black backgrounds, never notices distortion, and the illusion is that the pano really has a ceiling and floor, even though it doesn’t.
